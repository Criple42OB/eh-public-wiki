# Artificial Gravity Systems

## Overview
Artificial gravity aboard ships and space stations operates through distributed graviton-field generators integrated into the structural framework of the vessel or station. These systems are so ubiquitous and reliable that crew and passengers simply perceive them as ambient, much like the air you breathe. The technology has become invisible to everyday experience.

## How It Works

### The Technology
Each chamber, corridor, and compartment contains embedded graviton-field emitters that work in concert to create a uniform gravitational field oriented toward the designated "floor" of that space. The field strength can be adjusted per-sector, though most operational areas maintain Earth-standard gravity (1G) for crew comfort and efficiency.

The generators create a localized curvature effect without the mass required for natural gravity, drawing power from the ship or station's main reactors. This is why larger vessels can maintain gravity across entire decks simultaneously, the power requirements scale with surface area, not mass.

### Why It's Practical to Ignore
In normal operations, artificial gravity is so reliable and seamlessly integrated that there is no mechanical or psychological difference between standing on a planetary surface and standing on a ship's deck. Crew members don't experience drift, nausea, or any indication that they're not in a natural gravity well. The systems are engineered with massive redundancy; most vessels have gravity systems on backup power systems, backup systems on tertiary systems.

Thinking about gravity on a functioning ship is like thinking about the electromagnetic force holding your molecules together, it's there, essential, and utterly transparent to daily life.

## Damage and Failure

### Compromised Chambers
When a chamber or section suffers structural damage, the local graviton-field generators become unstable. If the damage is severe, the field can fail entirely, leaving that compartment in microgravity. This creates pockets of weightlessness within otherwise normal sections, which is both a hazard and occasionally useful for emergency repair crews or damage control.

### Cascade Failures
Catastrophic damage to a ship's reactor or main power distribution can cascade through the gravity network. Sections lose their fields in waves as power reroutes around damaged conduits. A heavily damaged vessel might have a patchwork of operational and zero-G compartments, creating a chaotic and dangerous environment for anyone trying to move through it.

### Backup Duration
Even with backup systems, isolated sections can only maintain artificial gravity for a limited time, hours at best, before battery reserves deplete. This is why ships in distress with critical power loss become increasingly hostile environments; crew members are suddenly dealing with unexpected weightlessness on top of other emergencies.

## Practical Implications
- **Ship Design**: The assumption of reliable gravity influences architecture, stairs, ladders, and railings are designed with downward orientation as primary. Without gravity, a ship becomes three-dimensional in ways its layout doesn't optimize for.
- **Crew Training**: Null-G training is standard for all crew members, precisely because damage scenarios are always a possibility. However, day-to-day operations assume gravity is present.
- **Station Life**: Massive space stations maintain gravity across entire sectors through redundant graviton networks. A station losing gravity is an extinction-level event, it means the station is either destroyed or has suffered a complete systemic failure.
